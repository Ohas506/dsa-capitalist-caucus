Founding Statement

Where We Stand

We are a caucus that takes an old and unfashionable position seriously: that voluntary exchange, private property, and the price system have done more to lift human beings out of poverty than any plan ever drafted in a committee room. What follows is where we stand, and the theory and history we stand on.

1 For Capitalism

We believe the truest expression of socialism is capitalism and all of its byproducts. We fundamentally reject the authoritarian nature of planned economies and instead embrace a system of collective commerce where people can exchange based on their wants and desires. The basic promise of socialism — an economy for the wants of the people, the many instead of the few — we believe is best realized by capitalism. If a society wants cheeseburgers, capitalism will produce more farms. Aristocracy says your rank in society should depend on your father — we say it should depend on if you can pitch investors on your AI dog food app.

"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." — Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, Book I (1776)

We do not romanticize wealth. We believe instead that capitalism best delivers on the socialist promise for an economy based on the need of the people, not based on the greed of a few. Under capitalism, there is a system for people to voluntarily distribute goods and services, empowering the proletariat — not the few greedy mercantilists at the top.

2 The Centrality of Voluntary Exchange

We believe that a socialist system can only exist through voluntary action — compulsion, a state monopoly on violence, is inherently anti-socialist in nature. From this belief we conclude a socialist economy must be based on the voluntary exchange of goods and services, and that the proletariat must bind together — as described by thinkers like Kropotkin — into a unity of bartering and trading, replaced in the modern age with money, to share and distribute services equitably, rather than them being divided up at the whims of a ruling class.

David Ricardo extended the argument to nations. In his Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817) he showed that even a country worse at producing everything still gains by specializing where its disadvantage is smallest — the ruling class that maintains state operations through violence produces an economy that caters solely to it, not the banded-together, sporadic freedom of the proletariat.

3 For Economic Freedom

We hold that economic freedom is not merely compatible with political freedom but is one of its preconditions. In Capitalism and Freedom (1962), Milton Friedman argued that a society which concentrates all economic power in the same hands — the state, and its authoritarianism — risks creating a system that caters to it, and its interests, the few, over the many, the working class and proletariat.

Friedrich Hayek made the sharper, darker version of the case in The Road to Serfdom (1944): that comprehensive central planning, however humane its intentions, tends to require a central planner, and the central planner tends to require obedience. We believe that any attempt to chart out a socialist centrally planned economy, or a socialist control over the economic engine of the working class, is fundamentally non-socialist, as it is based on compulsion and violence, instead of voluntary exchange and the collective decision-making of the working class.

4 The Entrepreneurial Road to Prosperity

We hold that the best way to lift the working class out of poverty is through the system of capitalism, and through a system that caters to the needs and interests of that class. We reject systems that attempt to alleviate poverty through centrally planned economies or directives — such systems, we believe, will merely preference one group of people over another, based on sex, nationality, or something else — stymieing the effort of class unity.

When Deng Xiaoping told China to "let some people get rich first" and permitted household farming and private enterprise after 1978, and when India dismantled the Licence Raj in 1991, hundreds of millions of people crossed out of destitution within a single generation.

5 Neoconservatism as True Socialism

We believe that the socialist project, the project of the left, went wrong after the takeover of Stalin, and his authoritarianism. We believe the truest thinker in opposition to Stalin was Leon Trotsky, and the Trotskyist movement. Further, we believe that true socialism is Trotskyism, and that the left's rejection of Trotskyism led it to be exiled from the left, and to take refuge on the other side — in the form of neoconservatism.

We contend that true socialism — Trotskyism — was carried through the 20th century as an intellectual refugee, taken in by the right as neoconservatism. Neoconservatism believes that ideas, morality — the moral core of the working class — should be what determines the economy, and the state, not a Stalinist system of central planners. Further, neoconservatism believes that the state should be the weapon of exporting this revolution.

"I regard myself as lucky to have been a young Trotskyist and I have not a single regret." — Irving Kristol, "Memoirs of a Trotskyist," The New York Times Magazine (1977)

The caucus draws the obvious conclusion. The internationalist impulse of the socialist left — its universalism, its impatience with the merely national, its faith that a better order can be built and exported — was always the movement's best instinct. We simply hold that its true content was liberal, commercial, and democratic all along. The neoconservative is, in Kristol's famous phrase, "a liberal who has been mugged by reality" — and, we would add, a Trotskyist who has been mugged by the price system. We hold that the torch of the left was carried through the 20th century in hiding on the right, by such thinkers as Kristol, and later leaders such as Cheney and Bush.

Our political home. The DSA Capitalist Caucus was built as a return of neoconservatism to its true origins — we are returning to our homeland, once more. We believe that with the right turning hostile to traditional neoconservative thinkers, the time is right for neoconservatism — true socialism — to return home once more, and for the left to reverse the shameful Stalinist purge of true socialism. The DSA, the electoral wing of such a movement, should follow in its footsteps.
